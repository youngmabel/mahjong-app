# 02. Tiles, Terms, and Why They're Called That

Content for the tutorial's tile lessons and the "why it's called that" notes, plus what each tile looks like (for our custom art) and a short history. The wording of each story matches how well it is documented; where an explanation is folklore, the tutorial should say so. Full research with every link: `research/tiles-terms-history.md`. Confidence tags: [H] high, [M] medium, [L] low.

**Stories to label as folklore if used at all** [L]: that the dragons come from archery (中 a hit, 發 a shot, 白 a miss); that the game is ancient or was played by Confucius (1920s marketing); that the name comes from tiles sounding like chattering sparrows; the "sparrow-catching reward" legend behind the 1 Bam.

## 2. The tiles, one by one

### 2.1 The set (152 tiles, no blanks)

108 suit tiles (three suits, 1 to 9, four of each) + 16 winds + 12 dragons + 8 flowers + 8 jokers = 152. That is the Chinese 144-tile set with 8 jokers added. Each player's wall is 19 stacks long and two high. [H] ([Sloper FAQ 7b](https://www.sloperama.com/majexchange/tutorial.html); [I Love Mahj](https://ilovemahj.com/mah-jongg-question/641/what-tiles-are-used-in-american-mah-jongg-and-how-many-of-each-are-there); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_mahjong))

Store-bought sets often include spares (blanks, extra jokers or flowers); this app uses exactly 152. A quick way to spot an American set: more than four tiles that say "Joker." [H] (Sloper FAQ 7b)

### 2.2 The money behind the suits

Mah jongg's suits come from Chinese money-suited card games (the *madiao* family): coins, strings of coins (a string held 100 coins), and "myriads" (10,000). Sloper calls the suits "Coins, Strings of Coins, and Myriads of Strings of Coins." [H] ([Sloper FAQ 18](https://sloperama.com/mjfaq/mjfaq18.html); [Sloper FAQ 11b](https://sloperama.com/mjfaq/mjfaq11b.html); [World of Playing Cards](https://wopc.co.uk/China/regional-cards-China-and-hong-kong)) Exactly how much a "myriad" stood for varies by source, so the safe line is "萬 means ten thousand; the suit stands for big sums of money." [L on the exact amount]

### 2.3 Bams

- Names: Bams, Bamboos, Sticks. Chinese 索子 suǒzi (索 = rope or cord), also 條子 tiáozi.
- Why "bamboo": the sticks are side views of coins threaded on a cord; Western players read the knots as bamboo joints. One source credits Babcock with the English name, but a rival 1922 rulebook already says "Bamboo." [H that they're strings; M-L on who named it] ([Wikipedia, Mahjong tiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahjong_tiles); [WOPC](https://wopc.co.uk/China/regional-cards-China-and-hong-kong); [Harr, *Pung Chow*, 1922](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/27318))
- **The 1 Bam bird**: an 1873 set shows a stylized string of coins; an 1890 set's is "very birdlike." The bird grew out of the single string. [H for the evolution] ([Sloper FAQ 7e](https://sloperama.com/mjfaq/special.html)) It is often called the sparrow; Japanese sets often use a peacock. Sloper doubts the sparrow pun has anything to do with it. In Mandarin play it's nicknamed 幺雞 yāojī, "little chicken." [M-L]

### 2.4 Craks

- Names: Craks (also Cracks, Characters, Wan). Chinese 萬 wàn (simplified 万), "ten thousand."
- Why "characters": it's the only suit whose value is written as a Chinese numeral rather than shown by counting symbols. [H]
- Face: Chinese numeral on top, red 萬 below. Numerals 1 to 9: 一 二 三 四 五 六 七 八 九. 1920s sets mostly use 万; sets from the 1970s on mostly use 萬. [H]

### 2.5 Dots

- Names: Dots, Circles, Balls. Chinese 筒子 tǒngzi; also 餅 bǐng ("cakes"), nicknamed 大餅 dàbǐng ("big pancake") for the 1 Dot.
- They depict copper "cash" coins, drawn as nested circles. The 5 Dot usually has a red center coin; the 1 Dot is often a single ornate, flower-like coin. [H] ([Stanwick, tile set history](https://www.themahjongtileset.co.uk/tile-set-history/mahjongg-before-and-after-mahjongg-part-1))

### 2.6 Winds

- Chinese 東 East, 南 South, 西 West, 北 North (originally "east wind" and so on). The traditional Chinese order is E, S, W, N; the card writes NEWS. [H]
- On the card, NEWS is four single tiles, not a kong, so no jokers and no calling for it except to win. [H]
- American play has no "prevailing wind" (a Chinese concept); the wind indicator in many sets gets used as the bettor's marker. [H] ([Sloper FAQ 7d](https://sloperama.com/mjfaq/bits.html))
- Tradition pairs each wind with a season (East spring, South summer, West autumn, North winter). [M, "it is said"]

### 2.7 Dragons

- "Dragons" is a Western name; Chinese players call them 三元牌 ("three fundamental tiles") or 箭牌 ("arrow tiles"). [H for the Chinese names; M-L that Babcock coined "Dragons"]
- **Red Dragon** 中 zhōng, "center" (in full 紅中, "red center"). Sloper suggests reading "center" as a fifth direction alongside the winds. Some sets mark it "C" (old romanization *chung*). [H meaning; M fifth direction]
- **Green Dragon** 發 fā, read as in 發財, "get rich," which is why Western books call it "fortune." Often marked "F" (Cantonese "faht"). [H]
- **White Dragon** 白 bái, "white, plain, blank"; also 白板 báibǎn, "white board." Originally blank; a rectangular frame was added when solid plastic tiles made a blank face hard to tell from a tile's back. **"Soap"**: it looked like a bar of soap to American players (a 1920s-era hand-carved set even shows a wrapped bar of soap). [M-H] ([Sloper FAQ 7e](https://sloperama.com/mjfaq/special.html); [Sloper FAQ 18](https://sloperama.com/mjfaq/mjfaq18.html))
- **Soap as zero**: Soap is the only tile ever used as zero, and when it serves as zero it has no suit. [H] Why Soap became zero isn't documented; the plain, "empty" face is the likely reason. [L] Year hands have been on the card since 1955.

### 2.8 Which dragon goes with which suit

**Red with Craks, Green with Bams, Soap with Dots.** The NMJL prints this pairing on the back of the card. [H] ([Sloper 2026 card FAQ](https://sloperama.com/mjfaq/2026.html))

- Why: not documented anywhere. The evident logic is color (Craks' 萬 is red, Bams are mostly green, which leaves Soap for Dots); teachers use mnemonics like "soap bubbles" for Dots. [L]
- It's an American convention, not a Chinese tradition. [M]
- **Teaching pitfall**: some manuals say "if you use Bams with dragons, the dragons must be green." Wrong for hands that call for opposite dragons. Teach the pairing as vocabulary and let each hand say "matching" or "opposite." [H]

### 2.9 Flowers

- Chinese 花牌 "flower tiles." Usually four flowers (the "Four Gentlemen": plum, orchid, chrysanthemum, bamboo) and four seasons (spring, summer, autumn, winter), numbered 1 to 4. Some sets use other themes. [H]
- **Why all eight are interchangeable**: in Chinese play flowers are seat-based bonus tiles. In the 1930s the American game made flowers wild; from its founding in 1937 the NMJL treated them as wild, so which seat a flower belonged to stopped mattering. When jokers took over the wild role in 1961, flowers stayed one interchangeable group, written "F" on the card. [M-H] ([Sloper FAQ 7e-F](https://sloperama.com/mjfaq/specialf.html); [Sloper column 509](https://sloperama.com/mahjongg/column509a.html))
- With eight flowers, flowers are the only tile that can form a quint (or sextet) without jokers. [H]

### 2.10 Jokers

- Introduced in 1961; before that, flowers served as wild tiles. [H] ([Sloper FAQ 19](https://sloperama.com/mjfaq/mjfaq19.html); [Sloper column 509](https://sloperama.com/mahjongg/column509a.html))
- How the count settled (Sloper's year-by-year compilation of what the card required) [M-H]: 1937 to 1942, 8 wild flowers and no jokers; wild-flower counts climbed as high as 24 in the 1950s; jokers arrived at 2, then 4, then 6, then 8; **8 flowers and 8 jokers since 1971**, so the set has been 152 tiles since 1971.
- Jokers barred from singles and pairs since about 1984 ("may have been," per Sloper). [M]
- Chinese sets had wild tiles before American jokers (for example 百搭, "a hundred uses"). Why the NMJL chose the word "Joker" isn't documented; it matches the wild card in a deck of cards. [L]

## 3. Glossary

| Term | Meaning in American play | Origin or note |
|---|---|---|
| Mah Jongg | The game; NMJL spelling (two words, double g) | Babcock's trademark spelling, filed 1923; "mahjong" became the generic word [H] |
| Mahj | Informal short form (the NMJL uses it) | [H] |
| "Mah Jongg!" | What you call out when you win | [H] |
| 麻雀 / 麻將 | Chinese names: máquè ("sparrow"), the older name; májiàng in Mandarin | Competing explanations; the "chattering sparrows" story is folklore [M/L] |
| Single, pair | One tile; two identical tiles. Never jokers | [H] |
| Pung | Three identical tiles | Chinese 碰 pèng, the call to claim a discard [H] |
| Kong | Four identical tiles | Chinese 槓 gàng [M] |
| Quint | Five identical tiles; needs jokers except with flowers | Plain English [H] |
| Sextet | Six identical tiles; needs jokers except with flowers | Older cards even had "septuplets" [H] |
| Chow | A three-tile run. **Not part of American play** (dropped in the 1930s) | Chinese 吃 chī "eat," or pidgin "chow-chow" (sources disagree) |
| Exposure | A pung, kong, quint, or sextet placed face up after claiming a discard | [H] |
| Concealed (C) / exposed (X) | Card markers: C hands expose nothing before winning; X hands may | [H] |
| Joker exchange (redeeming) | Swapping the real tile for a joker in an exposure, on your turn | [H] |
| Wall | Face-down tiles stacked two high, 19 stacks per player | [H] |
| Rack, pusher | The stand for your tiles; the arm or ruler that pushes the wall forward | Racks date to the 1920s [H] |
| East | The dealer, who starts with 14 tiles and discards first | No prevailing wind in American play [H] |
| NEWS | The four winds as written on the card; four singles | [H] |
| Honors | Winds and dragons together | [H] |
| Like numbers | The same number in different suits | [H] |
| Consecutive Run | Numbers in sequence; the card's most flexible section | [H] |
| Singles and Pairs | Card section of hands made only of singles and pairs; no jokers, no jokerless bonus | Once labeled "Singles & Pairs - No Bonus" [M-H] |
| Matching / opposite dragon | The dragon of a hand's suit / a dragon of a different suit | [H] |
| Soap | The White Dragon; also zero | [H] |
| F | A flower; all eight interchangeable | [H] |
| Charleston | Required passing before play: right, across, left; optional left, across, right | Name's origin unknown [L] |
| Courtesy pass | Optional 0 to 3 tile swap with the player across | [H] |
| Blind pass ("stealing") | Passing along tiles you just received without looking (first left and last right only) | [H] |
| Hot tile | A tile that another player likely needs | [H] |
| Dead hand | A hand that can no longer win or broke a rule; the player stops playing | [H] |
| Jokerless | A win with no jokers; doubles the payment (not for Singles and Pairs or Quints) | [M-H] |
| Self-pick | Winning on a tile you drew yourself | [M] |

## 4. What the tiles look like (for our custom art)

| Tile | Traditional face | What American sets add | Keep this so it stays recognizable |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bams 2 to 9 | Sticks in fixed counts; mostly green, often red accents on 1, 5, 7, 9 | Arabic numeral in a corner | The stick motif and exact count |
| 1 Bam | A bird (sparrow-like; peacock in Japanese sets) | "1" | One bird plus the index |
| Craks 1 to 9 | Chinese numeral on top, red 萬 below | Arabic numeral (essential for non-readers) | Red 萬 under a numeral |
| Dots 1 to 9 | Nested-circle coins in fixed counts; red center on the 5; ornate single coin on the 1 | Arabic numeral | The coin motif and exact count |
| Winds | 東 南 西 北 in black or blue | E, S, W, N (essential) | Character plus letter |
| Red Dragon | 中 in red | Sometimes a pictured dragon; "C" or "R" | Red plus 中 |
| Green Dragon | 發 in green | Sometimes a pictured dragon; "F" or "G" | Green plus 發 |
| White Dragon (Soap) | Blank or a rectangular frame | Sometimes "B," "P," or 白 | The frame or blank face |
| Flowers | Flowers, seasons, or other themed art, numbered 1 to 4 | Nothing standard | A picture tile clearly unlike suits and honors |
| Joker | The word "Joker" | Varied art | The word JOKER |

Notes [H unless marked] ([Sloper FAQ 7e](https://sloperama.com/mjfaq/special.html); [Sloper FAQ 7s](https://www.sloperama.com/mjfaq/mjfaq7s.html); [Wikipedia, Mahjong tiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahjong_tiles)):

- Corner indices (Arabic numerals and letters) were added for Western players around 1920; Craks and Winds need them most, since otherwise you must read Chinese characters.
- Tile proportions: a typical American tile is about 30 x 21 mm on its face, a width-to-height ratio of about 0.7.
- Exact dot and stick layouts have no published spec; check against a physical set (yours, when it arrives). [L]
- **Color blindness**: red and green dragons and the red/green suit colors are the risk. Real-world fixes include "R" and "G" stickers and sets that print the color names on the dragons. The app should never rely on color alone (see 08-design-ideas).
- Historian Annelise Heinz notes that "the recognizable tiles are what keep the game connected as a form of Mahjong," and that tile redesigns are currently contentious among some American manufacturers. So: original art, traditional anchors. [M] ([OPB interview, July 9, 2026](https://www.opb.org/article/2026/07/09/uo-professor-shares-history-of-mahjong/))
- Screen-reader labels can borrow the Unicode tile names (for example "MAHJONG TILE WHITE DRAGON"). [H]

## 5. A short history (for an optional "story of the game" screen)

- **China, mid-to-late 1800s.** Mah jongg took shape around Shanghai from older money-suited card games. It is not ancient; the oldest surviving sets date from the 1870s. Claims that Confucius played it were 1920s marketing. [H] ([Sloper FAQ 11](https://sloperama.com/mjfaq/mjfaq11.html); historian Annelise Heinz)
- **The 1920s American craze.** Joseph Babcock, a Standard Oil employee in China, wrote simplified English rules (the "Red Book," 1920), added English indices to the tiles, and trademarked "Mah-Jongg" (filed 1923). By 1923 to 1924 the game was a national fad, played even in the Harding White House; it faded by the Depression. [H] ([Sloper timeline](https://sloperama.com/mjfaq/mjfaq11h.html))
- **1937: the National Mah Jongg League.** Founded in New York to standardize the game, with Viola Cecil as president and Dorothy Meyerson as vice president. The League says it began with 32 members and now has more than 350,000. Its card limits the winning hands to the year's list, and a new card has come out each year since. Proceeds go to charity. [H; M that there was a card every single year] ([NMJL, The League](https://www.nationalmahjonggleague.org/league.aspx); [Heinz excerpt, Tablet](https://www.tabletmag.com/sections/community/articles/how-mahjong-became-jewish))
- **How the American game diverged**: chows dropped and flowers made wild (1930s); year hands (1955); jokers (1961); 8 flowers and 8 jokers, 152 tiles (1971); jokers barred from singles and pairs (about 1984). Sloper: the American game "was designed by women, to be enjoyed by women." The rules settled in the early 1960s. [H]
- **Leadership**: Ruth Unger led the League for more than 50 years until 2015; her sons David and Larry run it now. [H]
- **Fun fact**: "No other form of Mahjong in the world requires a card." (Heinz) [H]
